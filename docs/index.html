Building the Platform for
the Future of Medicine

How Mayo Clinic attempted to transform 157 years of complex clinical data into a technology platform — and what it means when a nonprofit decides to play by platform business rules.

56M Patient Lives in Network (2024) 81 Solution Developers 9 Institutional Partners, 4 Continents $17.9B 2023 Revenue (+10.2% YoY)

By Akshay Kumar  ·  Kogod School of Business, American University  ·  View on GitHub ↗

The Business Problem

A 157-Year Institution. A Platform-Business Bet.

Mayo Clinic's most valuable asset — decades of complex, rare-disease patient data — was sitting in institutional archives. The question was not whether AI could use it. The question was whether Mayo could build a platform around it without betraying the patients and mission that created it.

"When I look at the 40 years that I've been in this industry … I've often looked at 'when is there a perfect storm for innovation?'"

— Dr. John Halamka, HealthLeaders Media, 2020 ↗

When Dr. John Halamka took over as the inaugural President of Mayo Clinic Platform on January 1, 2020, he inherited both an extraordinary opportunity and a structural contradiction. Mayo's clinical depth — genomic sequences paired with 30-year patient trajectories, physician notes capturing the texture of medical reasoning across four decades of hard cases — was genuinely unlike anything in commercial healthcare.

Other health systems had more patients. None had this depth of complexity. And it was precisely the complexity — the rare diseases, the edge cases, the unsolved puzzles — that made Mayo's data most valuable for training AI that needed to learn from the hardest problems, not just the most common ones.

Halamka had three months to begin building that platform before COVID-19 arrived in March 2020 — turning the abstract question of what a hospital could do with its data into the most urgent question in global health.

The Protagonist

Dr. John Halamka: The Right Person for an Impossible Job

Halamka was unusual enough that he made something healthcare had needed for decades but rarely produced: a clinician who actually understood what the algorithms were doing.

Dr. John Halamka
President, Mayo Clinic Platform
Joined January 1, 2020
Emergency Medicine MD Computer Scientist Stanford (Dual Degree) MD/PhD — UCSF/UC Berkeley Harvard Medical School National Academy of Medicine HITECH Act Advisor 8 Books on Health IT
Former CIO, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (20 years). Federal health IT advisor under Bush and Obama administrations. Started his first software company at age 13.

Halamka had watched expensive health IT systems get deployed, clinicians resist them, and promised outcomes never materialize — not because the technology was wrong, but because the humans around it hadn't been prepared for what it would demand of them.

His prescription for Mayo Clinic Platform was therefore not primarily technical. It was organizational: build a platform that convened the right people around a shared resource, with governance architecture strong enough to sustain trust across institutions, companies, patient communities, and regulators — simultaneously.

"It isn't technology; technology is good enough. It's fear of change."

— Dr. John Halamka, HealthLeaders Media, 2020 ↗

By 2024, Halamka was governing a platform ten times the size of the one he had designed the governance rules for in 2020. The question was not whether the platform was succeeding. The question was whether the structures he had built five years earlier were built for what it had become.

Strategic Decisions

Three Decisions. Two Tracks. One Bet.

Mayo Clinic Platform was built around three interconnected strategic choices. Together they represent a deliberate two-track AI strategy that Halamka designed as complementary — not competing.

Decision 01  ·  September 2019 ↗

Google Partnership: The Data Enclave

A 10-year strategic alliance with Google Cloud built the "data under glass" architecture: a federated enclave where third parties can train algorithms on Mayo's de-identified patient data without that data ever leaving Mayo's custody. Algorithms come in; trained models go out. Patient data never moves.

Key safeguard: Bin-size-of-10 de-identification standard — any output must be attributable to at least 10 individuals. A multi-stakeholder DaTA Board (11 community members) reviews all access requests.

Decision 02  ·  2022–2024

The Distributed Network: Federated at Scale

Rather than centralizing all relevant health data at Mayo — logistically impossible and politically radioactive — Halamka built a federated network of partner institutions. Each kept physical and legal control of its own data; only analytical insights crossed institutional boundaries.

By June 2024: 9 partners across 4 continents, 56 million patient lives, expanding from Mercy Health (US) to Albert Einstein (Brazil), Sheba Medical (Israel), and University Health Network (Canada).

Decision 03  ·  September 2023 ↗

Microsoft Partnership: AI in the Workflow

Mayo became one of the first healthcare organizations to deploy Microsoft 365 Copilot. This was not about diagnostics — it was about physician burnout. Surveys showed clinicians spending over half their working hours on documentation rather than patient care.

Track 1 (Google): Clinical diagnostic AI — better diagnoses. Track 2 (Microsoft): Productivity AI — physicians who are rested enough to pay attention to what Track 1 shows them.

Platform_Accelerate

Launched in 2022 as a 20-week (later 30-week) startup incubation program. Startups get access to de-identified clinical data and expert mentorship; in return, their algorithms are validated against real patient populations at a scale they could never access independently. First cohort (2022): 4 companies. By 2025: 45+ startups across multiple cohorts.

Case Study in Practice — ELEFT (March 2024)

FDA cleared ELEFT — the Eko Low Ejection Fraction Tool — in March 2024: a stethoscope that uses AI co-developed with Mayo Clinic to detect heart failure indicators from cardiac sounds alone, without requiring an echocardiogram. Algorithm developed and validated inside Mayo's data enclave. Product brought to market by Eko Health. The "wisdom" leaves the enclave. The patient data does not.

Diagnostic Analytics

Why Watson Failed — and What Mayo Did Differently

IBM Watson Health is the structural comparison threaded through this case. Understanding the specific failure modes illuminates every governance choice Mayo made.

Dimension IBM Watson Health (2011–2022) Mayo Clinic Platform (2020–2024)
Training Data Source Single institution (Memorial Sloan Kettering). Did not generalize. Federated network across 9 institutions, 56M patient lives, 4 continents.
Validation Method Physician-annotated cases. Optimized to agree with oncologists, not improve outcomes. Real-world patient population validation inside data enclave. FDA clearance required before clinical use.
Governance Structure No independent oversight. No community representation. No accountability mechanism. Multi-stakeholder DaTA Board (11 community members). "One Table" cross-functional review. IRB-equivalent oversight.
Explainability Black-box. Physicians couldn't understand recommendations; couldn't calibrate trust. Explainability is a design requirement. Algorithms deployed as decision support, not decision replacement.
Commercial Pressure Capabilities marketed before clinical evidence existed. IBM sold to hospital executives, not researchers. FDA clearance bar maintained. Platform_Accelerate validates before deployment, not after sale.
Outcome Sold to Francisco Partners (private equity), 2022. MD Anderson: $62M project discontinued 2017. ELEFT cleared FDA, March 2024. Foundation model collaboration with Microsoft Research and Cerebras, January 2025.
Scenario Analysis

Three Futures for Mayo Clinic Platform (2025–2030)

These are analytical constructs, not forecasts. Each scenario rests on a set of stated assumptions about how key variables resolve. The purpose is to identify which assumptions are load-bearing — not to predict which scenario will occur.

Scenario Core Assumptions Likely Trajectory Key Risk / Limiting Factor
A: Governance Compounds DaTA Board legitimacy holds. Partner trust sustained. FDA regulatory clarity improves for AI SaMD. Foundation models generate measurable clinical outcomes. Network effects accelerate. Developer ecosystem reaches 150+. Foundation models trained on Mayo data become a durable competitive moat. Governance overhead becomes operational bottleneck. Multi-homing by developers limits lock-in. Revenue model remains opaque to partners.
B: Commercial Pressure Builds Startup iteration demands increase. Epic App Orchard / Google Med-PaLM offer cheaper validation alternatives. Mayo faces talent competition from Big Tech. Mayo risks becoming a certification service rather than a platform. Developers complete validation, then route around Mayo in subsequent product iterations. The "convener" model sacrifices long-term revenue capture for short-term legitimacy. If Mayo cannot retain developers post-validation, network value leaks.
C: Regulatory or Trust Disruption A high-profile AI clinical error at a Platform partner. Regulatory action on HIPAA or nonprofit commercial activity. Patient advocacy challenge to data governance model. Platform growth pauses for governance review. Community board legitimacy tested. Some institutional partners exit the network. Watson Health precedent: regulatory and public trust failures are hard to recover from. The Mayo brand — the platform's core asset — is also its primary vulnerability.

All scenarios based on structural features described in publicly available case evidence. Not financial projections.

Risk Communication

Strategic Tension Severity

Qualitative risk assessment of the four strategic tensions identified in the case, rated by potential impact if unresolved.

High Impact

Mission vs. Revenue

Nonprofit identity + platform logic are structurally incompatible. Revenue model opacity increases regulatory and advocacy exposure over time.

High Impact

Institutional Identity vs. Platform Logic

Platform businesses extract value from network participants. Mayo's brand depends on not being seen as extracting value from healthcare.

Medium Impact

Speed vs. Safety

Governance timelines will be tested as the developer ecosystem scales. Watson failed partly because commercial pressure outran clinical validation.

Medium Impact

Openness vs. Competitive Advantage

Market Sizing & Opportunity Framing

Mayo's Position in a $208B Market

The global AI in healthcare market was valued at $22.45B in 2023 and is projected to reach $208.23B by 2030 (industry estimate, ~37.5% CAGR). Mayo Clinic Platform is not attempting to capture that entire market. Its model — federated validation + clinical brand credibility — addresses a specific and structurally defensible segment: the AI validation and clinical credentialing bottleneck that every health AI company must pass through to reach FDA clearance and hospital adoption.

Every algorithm that needs FDA clearance for clinical deployment needs either (a) access to diverse, complex real-world patient data to train on, or (b) validation evidence from a clinically credible institution that regulators and hospital procurement teams trust. Mayo Clinic Platform provides both. No technology company — not Google, not Microsoft, not Epic — has the same combination of clinical brand trust and complex-case data depth.

The question the case does not answer: at what point does Mayo's revenue from this position justify the institutional risk of the platform strategy? The financial architecture of Platform — its revenue terms with solution developers, the commercial return from the Google and Microsoft partnerships, the compensation structure for the ELEFT-style validations — has not been publicly disclosed. For a platform asking institutional partners to trust its governance of 56 million patient lives, that opacity is a compounding risk.

The Open Questions

Four Tensions. No Tidy Resolutions.

By 2024, Mayo Clinic Platform had achieved scale that would have seemed improbably ambitious as a five-year goal in 2020. But scale amplifies tensions that governance structures were built for smaller numbers.

Tension 1

Speed vs. Safety

Health AI startups iterate weekly. Mayo's clinical governance runs on months-long review cycles. Startups in Platform_Accelerate sometimes chafed at the pace. Mayo's clinical leadership sometimes worried that commercial pressure was pushing validation faster than the evidence warranted. IBM Watson is the cautionary tale everyone invokes.

Tension 2

Mission vs. Revenue

Mayo Clinic Platform's financial architecture is not publicly disclosed. Solution developer fees, institutional partner terms, and the commercial return from Google and Microsoft are folded into overall institutional income. For a nonprofit with IRS-exempt status, commercial platform revenue carries regulatory and legitimacy risk that increases with scale.

Tension 3

Openness vs. Competitive Advantage

The distributed data network requires deep trust from institutional partners. But Mayo competes with some of those partners for patients, research funding, and top physicians. The "One Table" governance model was designed to manage this; whether it sustains trust across nine institutions on four continents, with 81 solution developers and two Big Tech partnerships, remains open.

Tension 4

Institutional Identity vs. Platform Logic

Halamka described Platform's goal as transforming Mayo into a "digital data business." Platform businesses operate by a logic — grow the network, monetize interactions, extract value from ecosystem participants — that sits in inherent tension with the values that made Mayo's brand worth anything in the first place.

"Platforms represent our greatest opportunity to bring real, lasting, and transformative change to our global healthcare system."

— Dr. Gianrico Farrugia, Mayo Clinic CEO, PlatforMed 2024 ↗

For the Classroom

Discussion Questions

These questions are designed to generate genuine debate — not to have correct answers. Each surfaces a real tradeoff with defensible positions on multiple sides.

  1. Dr. Halamka described Platform's goal as turning Mayo into a "digital data business." Is this description compatible with Mayo's nonprofit mission? Who should draw the line — and how?
  2. The "data under glass" model was designed to prevent patient data from leaving Mayo's custody. What are the governance risks of this model as it scales to 56M patient lives across 9 institutions on 4 continents?
  3. Halamka argued that "change management is always the hardest task" — not the technology. What specific change management challenges arise in deploying AI tools to physicians at a 157-year-old institution? How would you address them?
  4. IBM Watson Health failed and was divested in 2022. What structural factors differentiate Mayo Clinic Platform's AI strategy from Watson's? Are those factors sufficient?
  5. Halamka said: "If your doctor can be replaced by AI, then your doctor should be replaced by AI." What is the strongest argument in its favor? The strongest argument against?
  6. Mayo competes with some of its distributed data network partners for patients, talent, and research funding. How should Mayo manage this conflict of interest? What governance mechanisms would you recommend — and what should their limits be?
  7. Platform_Accelerate gives startups access to Mayo's patient data and clinical brand in exchange for validation work. Who benefits more — Mayo or the startups? Is the exchange equitable?
  8. Mayo's 2023 capital expenditure was $1.18 billion, much of it for physical campus expansion. How should Mayo balance physical infrastructure investment against digital platform investment? Is there a tension between the two strategic directions?
